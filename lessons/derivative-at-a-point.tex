\medskip
The derivative quantifies this qualitative difference.  

\begin{definition} \label{def:derivative-at-a-point}
  Let \(f(x)\) be a function defined on an open interval containing some constant \(a\). 
  
  The \hlmain{derivative of \(f(x)\) at \(a\)}, denoted by \hlmain{\(f'(a)\)} is defined by
  \begin{align} 
    \label{eq:def-derivative-1}
    f'(a) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \\
    \intertext{or equivalently}
    \label{eq:def-derivative-2}
    f'(a) &= \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}
  \end{align}
  provided that the limit is a finite number. The two limits are related by a change of variable \(x = a + h\) or \(h = x - a\). 

  The \hlmain{tangent line for \(f(x)\) at \(x = a\)} is the line with slope \(f'(a)\) and passes through the point \((a, f(a))\). Its equation is
  \[
    y = \underbrace{f'(a)}_{\text{slope}} (x - a) + f(a), \quad\text{if \(f'(a)\) is a finite number.}
  \]

  \textbook{page 217}
\end{definition}

% \faStar{} Geometrically, the number \(f'(a)\) represents the slope of the curve \(y = f(x)\) at the point \((a, f(a))\) if \(f'(a)\) exists. 

Terminology: If \(f'(a)\) is a \emph{finite number}, then \(f\) is said to be \hlmain{differentiable at \(a\)}. If \(f'(a)\) is not a finite number, then \(f\) is said to be \hlmain{not differentiable at \(a\)}.
\clearpage

The limit definition of derivatives, e.g. Equation~\eqref{eq:def-derivative-1} or \eqref{eq:def-derivative-2}, is fundamentally important and \hlwarn{useful for unfamiliar problems}. If you know differentiation rules, the exercises in this section may seem overly complicated. However, it is the purpose of this section to help you understand that, fundamentally, the derivative is a limit. Everything you know about limits can be used to solve differentiation problems, especially the unfamiliar ones.  \label{comment:derivative-definition}

Differentiability is fundamentally dependent on continuity. 
\begin{thm} \label{thm:differentiability-implies-continuity}
  If \(f\) is \hlsupp{differentiable at \(a\)}, then \(f\) is \hlmain{continuous at \(a\)} and, consequently, \hlmain{\(f(a)\) must be defined}.

  If differentiability at \(a\) is a PB\&J, then continuity at \(a\) and \(f(a)\) are like peanut butter and jam. Without continuity at \(a\), a function cannot be differentiable at \(a\).

  \textbook{Theorem 3.1 on page 237}
\end{thm}

\begin{example} \label{eg:find-a-and-b-differentiability}
  Find all values of \(a\) and \(b\) such that
  \(
  f(x) =
  \begin{cases}
    a x^{2}, &\text{if } x \le 1 \\
    b x - 2,  &\text{if } x > 1
  \end{cases}
  \)
  is differentiable at \(x = 1\).

\end{example}
