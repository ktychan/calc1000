The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) is the mathematical description of patterns like the above.

\begin{thm}[Intermediate Value Theorem] \label{thm:ivt}
  Let \(f\) be a \underline{\hspace{1in}} function over a closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\). If \(z\) is any real number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), then \underline{\hspace{1in}} a number \(c\) in \([a,b]\) satisfying \(f(c) = z\).

  \textbook{Theorem 2.11 on page 188}
\end{thm}

Note that \emph{closed and bounded} means an interval must have a finite length and include both endpoints. For example \([1,\infty)\) and \((1,3]\) are not closed and bounded intervals.
\clearpage

\faStar{} The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) is \hlmain{useful} when we wish to predict whether an equation has at least one solution but do not need to find their exact expression.

To apply IVT, we must successfully complete three tasks: 
\begin{enumerate}[wide, label=(IVT \arabic*)]
  \item Verify that we have a \hlmain{closed and bounded} interval \([a,b]\).
  \item Verify that {\(f\) is \hlmain{continuous} on \([a,b]\)}.
  \item Verify that {\(z\) is \hlmain{between} \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\)}. You may wish to sketch this.
\end{enumerate}
If we cannot complete one or more of these tasks, then IVT \hlwarn{does not tell us anything}.

When we apply the IVT to predict the existence of a solution, it's always easier to move everything to the left-hand side to get an equation of the form \(f(x) = 0\) and apply IVT to \(f(x)\) over the specified interval.

\begin{example}
  Show the function \(f(t) = -t^{5} + t^{2} - 1\) has a root between \(-1\) and \(1\).

\end{example}
