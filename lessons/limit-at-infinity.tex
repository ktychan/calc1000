\begin{definition}[horizontal asymptote]
  If a constant \(L\) satisfies 
  \[
    L = \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) \quad\text{or}\quad L = \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x),
  \]
  then the horizontal line \(y = L\) is called a \hlmain{horizontal asymptote} for \(f(x)\).

  \textbook{page 408}
\end{definition}

We do not cover oblique asymptotes in \thiscourse{}.

We now evaluate limits at infinity algebraically. The following facts will serve us well.

  \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}l@{\hspace{2em}}c@{}}
      \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} x^{(\text{positive constant})} = \lim_{x \to \infty} e^{\text{(positive constant)} x} = \infty\)
        & \raisebox{-0.5\height}{\includestandalone[page=1]{../standalones/plot-limits-at-infinity-facts}} \\[4ex]
        \(\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x^{(\text{positive constant})}} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{e^{\text{(positive constant)} x}} = 0\)
        & \raisebox{-0.5\height}{\includestandalone[page=2]{../standalones/plot-limits-at-infinity-facts}} \\
    \end{tabular}
  \end{center}

  Moreover,
  \(\lim_{x \to \infty} (\text{polynomial}) = (\text{the sign of the coefficient for \(x^{\text{largest power}}\)}) \, \infty\).
  
\end{mdframed}

\label{page:limits-at-infinity-strategy}
\faStar{} All limit laws apply to limits at infinity, but we often need to reorganize functions by \hlmain{factoring out their dominant (fastest-growing) terms together with their coefficients}. This technique is called \hlmain{analyzing dominant terms}.

Here is the technique in its simplest form. Factor the largest powers of \(x\) together with its coefficient out of the numerator and out of the denominator. To factor \(x^{a}\) out of \(x^{b}\), we do \(\frac{x^{b}}{x^{a}}\) and simplify. Lastly, apply the above facts.

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
  \begin{align*}
    \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{x^{4} + 5x}}{2x - 1}
  &= \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{x^{2}\sqrt{1 + \frac{5}{x^{3}}}}{2x\left( 1 - \frac{1}{2x} \right)} \\[3ex]
  &= \lim_{x \to \infty} \left( \frac{x}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{1 + \frac{5}{x^{3}}}}{1 - \frac{1}{2x}} \right) \\[3ex]
  &= \infty.
  \end{align*}
\end{minipage}

The calculation details for \(\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{1 + \frac{5}{x^{3}}}}{1 - \frac{1}{2x}} = \frac{1+0}{1-0}\) is not shown.

\faStar{} A \hlmain{change of variable} \(u = -x\) turns limits at negative infinity to limits at positive infinity:
\[
  \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = \lim_{u \to \infty} f(-u).
\]
This little trick is not in the textbook but helps avoid subtle sign mistakes while analyzing dominant terms at \(-\infty\), \hlsupp{especially for functions with even roots}. 


\begin{example}
  Evaluate \(\lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{\sqrt{x^{2} + 1}}{x + 1}\).

\end{example}
