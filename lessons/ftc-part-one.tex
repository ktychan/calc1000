\section{\enquote{Accumulation functions}}

Recall we have been interpreting the integrand in \(\int_{a}^{b} f(t) \;dt\) as a rate of change. We informally call a function of this form
\[
  F(x) = \int_{\text{constant}}^{x} f(t) \;dt
\]
an \enquote{accumulation function} because it accumulates a rate of change in the following sense.

\section{The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, part 1}
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus has two parts. Part 1 tells us how to differentiate an \enquote{accumulation function}.

\begin{thm} \label{thm:ftc-part-one}
  If \(f(x)\) is continuous over \([a,b]\), then the function \(F(x)\) defined by
  \[
    F(x) = \int_{\text{constant}}^{x} f(t) \;dt
  \]
  is differentiable on \((a,b)\) and \(F'(x) = f(x)\).

  \textbook{Theorem 5.4: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus Part 1 on page 552}
\end{thm}
\faExclamationTriangle{} The constant in FTC part 1 must be the lower bound of the definite integral!
