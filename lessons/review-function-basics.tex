Sophisticated functions are built by iteratively combining basic functions using operations:
\[
  f+g \hspace{0.75in}
  f-g \hspace{0.75in}
  f \cdot g \hspace{0.75in}
  f/g \hspace{0.75in}
  f \circ g \hspace{0.75in}
  f(x) = \begin{cases} \cdots \\ \cdots \\ \cdots \end{cases}
\]
\blanklines{5}

\faExclamationTriangle{}
Note \(f \cdot g\) and \(f \circ g\) are not always the same function! Most of the time they are not the same.

\begin{exercise} 
  For each category of functions, write down two or three examples and at least one non-example (something that looks like it should fit but really doesn't).

  \faStar{} Recalling example only targets the lowest level of learning and cognition.  However, \hlmain{generating} examples and non-examples without any aids develops \hlmain{sharpen your understanding} of relevant concepts.

  \begin{itemize}[wide]
    \item A \hlmain{zero} of a function \(f(x)\) is a \hlsupp{number \(r\)} (in its domain) so that \(f(r) = 0\).  Graphically, zeros of a function are also called its \hlmain{\underline{\hspace{1in}} intercept} or \hlmain{\underline{\hspace{1in}}}.

      \blanklines{5}

    \item A \hlmain{root function} is a power function of the form \(x^{1/n}\) or \(\sqrt[n]{x}\) where \(n\) is a \hlsupp{positive} integer. 

      \blanklines{5}

    \item A \hlmain{rational power} is a power function of the form \(x^{p/q}\) where \(p,q\) are integers but \(q \ne 0\).

      \blanklines{5}

    \item Functions that involve \emph{only} \(+, -, \times, \div\), roots and rational powers are called \hlmain{algebraic functions}. 

      \blanklines{5}

    \item A \hlmain{transcendental function} is a function that is not an algebraic function.

      \blanklines{5}

  \end{itemize}
\end{exercise}
