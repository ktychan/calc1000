\subsection{Net area} \label{subsec:defint-meaning} \updatenote{Was \enquote{Definite integrals as area under the curve}. Why: the subsection builds up net area first, and the definite integral is introduced as its notation.}
The geometric idea of \hlmain{area} is very flexible and widely applicable in science. In each example below, a quantity changes at a rate that is constant on each piece of time, and the rate can be negative. \updatenote{New sentence. In each case, the answer comes from the rectangles between the graph of the rate and the horizontal axis: add their areas, counting area \hlwarn{below the axis as negative}. This signed total is the \hlmain{net area}. \updatenote{New paragraph. Why: it names net area using piecewise constant rates, before the definite integral.}

It is scientifically important to ask: What if \(v(t)\), \(r(t)\) or \(c(t)\) is not piecewise constant? Can we still figure out the displacement, the change in medicine and the change in the account balance? \updatenote{Was \enquote{What if \(v(t)\) or \(r(t)\) is not a constant function? Can we still figure out the displacement and the amount of medicine administered?} Why: the examples are now piecewise constant, and the accounting example is new.}

Net area still makes sense: the region between the graph and the horizontal axis is no longer made of rectangles, but we can still add the area above the axis and subtract the area below it.
\begin{definition}[net area] \label{def:net-area}
  The \hlmain{net area bounded by \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = 0\)} \hlsupp{over the interval \([a,b]\)} is
  \begin{align*}
    \text{net area} = {}& \bigg(\text{area below \(y = f(x)\) and above \(y = 0\)}\bigg) \\
    & - \bigg(\text{area above \(y = f(x)\) and below \(y = 0\)}\bigg).
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}
\updatenote{New definition, built from the net area formula that used to follow the definition of the definite integral. Why: net area now comes first, so the definite integral can be introduced as its notation.}

\begin{center}
  \includestandalone[page=4]{../standalones/plot-net-area-motivation}
\end{center}

The definite integral is the notation for net area, and it answers the question above for all functions. \updatenote{Replaces \enquote{Both scenarios, and countless more, are asking the same fundamental geometric question:} and \enquote{The definite integral answers this question, and it is defined for all functions, not just the non-negative ones.} Why: the lesson now goes directly from net area to the definite integral. The GeoGebra link is now in Subsection~\ref{subsec:approximating-area}.}

Behind the scenes, a definite integral is a \hlmain{limit of Riemann sums}: we approximate the area with many thin rectangles, add up their areas, and let the rectangles get thinner and thinner. This is the theoretical underpinning of the definite integral, and we will come back to it in Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-formal}. Until then, we work with definite integrals as area. \updatenote{New paragraph. Why: it tells students up front that definite integrals are defined through Riemann sums, so the area interpretation in the next pages is not the whole story, and it points ahead to the formal definition.}

\begin{definition} \label{def:definite-integral-net-area}
  The \hlmain{definite integral of a function \(f(x)\) \hlsupp{from \(a\) to \(b\)}}, written \(\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx\), represents the \hlmain{net area bounded by \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = 0\)} \hlsupp{over the interval \([a,b]\)}.  Sometimes, it is also called the net signed area.

  \updatenote{The limit formula and \enquote{provided that the limit exists} moved to Definition~\ref{def:definite-integral} in Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-formal}. Why: the formula needs Riemann sums and Sigma notation, which now come later, and the examples below need only the net area meaning.}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Properties of definite integrals} \label{subsec:defint-properties}
We now study properties of definite integrals. \updatenote{Removed \enquote{Because the definite integral is defined in terms of a summation, all but one of its properties mimic those of summation properties}, which now comes near the end of Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-formal}. Why: this subsection explains the properties with the area interpretation alone, and summations come later.}

Definite integrals have a ``flip'' property (non-standard terminology). \updatenote{Removed \enquote{which \hlwarn{does not} have a counterpart in summations}. That comparison now comes near the end of Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-formal}. Why: summations are not introduced until Subsection~\ref{subsec:sigma}.}

\begin{thm}
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral-flip}
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = - \int_{b}^{a} f(x) \;dx.
  \end{equation}

  \textbook{Equation (5.10) on page 538}
\end{thm}

\begin{example}[time reversal] \label{ex:flip-time-reversal}
  Recall the object from the first motivating example on page~\pageref{item:velocity-example}. Its displacement from \(t = 1\) to \(t = 8\) minutes is \(\int_{1}^{8} v(t) \;dt\).

  Now play a video of the trip in reverse, so that time runs from \(t = 8\) back to \(t = 1\). The object retraces its path and ends where it started. The displacement of this rewound trip is \(\int_{8}^{1} v(t) \;dt\).

  \begin{center}
    \includestandalone[page=5]{../standalones/plot-net-area-motivation}
  \end{center}

  The two displacements have the same size but opposite directions, so
  \[
    \int_{8}^{1} v(t) \;dt = \underline{\hspace{1.5cm}} \int_{1}^{8} v(t) \;dt = \underline{\hspace{1.5cm}} \text{ km}.
  \]

  \blanklines{5}
\end{example}
\updatenote{New example, with a sketch, in place of the blank space after the flip property. Why: rewinding the trip from the first motivating example gives the flip property a physical meaning. Integrating from \(b\) to \(a\) runs time backwards, which reverses the direction of the displacement.}

\begin{thm}
  \begin{equation}
    \int_{a}^{a} f(x) \;dx = \underline{\hspace{1cm}}.
  \end{equation}

\end{thm}

Definite integrals also have a split/merge property with \hlmain{no restriction} at all. \updatenote{Was \enquote{Recall summations have a split/merge property \ldots{} restricted to \(1 \le m \le n\). \begin{thm}
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral-split}
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \;dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \;dx.
  \end{equation}

  The number \(c\) can be anything, even outside the interval \([a,b]\), as long as both integrals on the right are well-defined.

  \textbook{Equation (5.14) on page 538}
\end{thm}

\begin{thm}
  \begin{align*}
    \int_{a}^{b} (\text{constant}) f(x) \;dx &= (\text{constant}) \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx && \text{(constant multiple)}\\[1ex]
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx + \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \;dx &= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) + g(x) \;dx && \text{(sum)} \\[1ex]
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx - \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \;dx &= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) - g(x) \;dx && \text{(difference)}
  \end{align*}

  \textbook{Equations (5.13), (5.11) and (5.12) on page 538}
\end{thm}

\subsection{Approximating area} \label{subsec:approximating-area}
So far, we evaluated definite integrals using shapes with known area formulas. What about \(\int_{0}^{1} x^{2} \;dx\), the area under a parabola? The rest of this lesson builds a precise way to evaluate definite integrals like this one, starting with approximation: \updatenote{New question and sentence, replacing \enquote{We will do so using approximation}. Why: approximation and Riemann sums now come after the area meaning and the properties, as the precise way to evaluate a definite integral when no area formula applies.}
\url{https://www.geogebra.org/calculator/bsdvzx3f}

It turns out that approximation is the theoretically advantageous starting point for finding an exact answer: If we approximate an area using \underline{\hspace{2in}} many rectangles, then we get the exact area.

To approximate the area under \(y = f(x)\) over \([a,b]\) using \(n\) rectangles, we use the following notation. \label{page:riemann-notation} \updatenote{This list was under the Riemann sum definition. Why: the five methods below use its notation, and the definition now comes later. The item \enquote{\(n\) is a constant inside the summation} stays with the definition.}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The interval \([a,b]\) is divided into sub-intervals by points \(x_{0}, x_{1}, x_{2}, \dots, x_{n}\). \updatenote{"sub-interval" corrected to "sub-intervals".}
  \item \(n\) represents the number of sub-intervals of the interval \([a,b]\). \updatenote{"sub-interval" corrected to "sub-intervals".}
  \item \(\Delta x\) is a single symbol and means \(\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}\).
  \item \(\Delta x\) represents the width of each sub-interval.
  \item The \(i\)-th interval is \([x_{i-1}, x_{i}]\).
  \item \(x_{i}^{*}\) is a \hlmain{choice} inside the \(i\)th sub-interval.
  \item The \(i\)-th rectangle has width \(\Delta x\) and height \(f(x_{i}^{*})\), so the approximation is \(f(x_{1}^{*}) \Delta x + f(x_{2}^{*}) \Delta x + \dots + f(x_{n}^{*}) \Delta x\). \updatenote{New item. Why: it writes the approximation as a sum, which Subsection~\ref{subsec:sigma} shortens with Sigma notation.}
\end{enumerate}

The freedom to choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) gives us useful approximation techniques. \updatenote{"give" corrected to "gives" to agree with the singular subject "freedom".} Here are five simple methods.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{\(L_{n}\) Left-endpoint approximation}: Choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be \(x_{i-1}\) the left endpoint of the \(i\)-th interval.
  \item \textbf{\(R_{n}\) Right-endpoint approximation}: Choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be \(x_{i}\) the right endpoint of the \(i\)-th interval.
  \item \textbf{\(M_{n}\) Mid-endpoint approximation}: Choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be the midpoint of the \(i\)-th interval.
  \item \textbf{Upper sum}: Choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be the number in the \(i\)-th interval at which \(f(x)\) is maximized.
  \item \textbf{Lower sum}: Choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be the number in the \(i\)-th interval at which \(f(x)\) is minimized.
\end{enumerate}
For the upper and lower sums, the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees such choices exist.

\begin{thm}
  \label{page:summation-properties}
  Let \(c\) be a constant. Let \(a_{1}, \dots, a_{n}, b_{1}, \dots, b_{n}\) be two lists of numbers.
  \begin{align*}
    \sum_{i=1}^{n} {\color{attn} \mathbf{c}} \; a_{i}
    &= {\color{attn} \mathbf{c}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i}
    &&\text{(constant multiple)} \\[6ex]
    \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} {\color{attn}\,\mathbf{+}\,} \sum_{i=1}^{n} b_{i}
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (a_{i} {\color{attn}\,\mathbf{+}\,}\, b_{i})
    &&\text{(sum)} \\[6ex]
    \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} {\color{attn}\,\mathbf{-}\,} \sum_{i=1}^{n} b_{i}
    &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (a_{i} {\color{attn}\,\mathbf{-}\,} b_{i})
    && \text{(difference)} \\[6ex]
    \sum_{i=1}^{{\color{attn}\mathbf{n}}} a_{i}
    &=
    \sum_{i=1}^{{\color{attn}\mathbf{m}}} a_{i} +
    \sum_{i={\color{attn}\mathbf{m+1}}}^{{\color{attn}\mathbf{n}}} a_{i}
    && \text{(split/merge a sum)} \\[6ex]
  \end{align*}

  \textbook{Rule: Properties of Sigma Notation, page 509}
\end{thm}

To \hlmain{simplify} or to \hlmain{evaluate} a summation written in Sigma notation means to perform the specified addition.  The formulas in Equation~\eqref{formula:summations} are useful and examinable.
\begin{equation} \label{formula:summations}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n} i = {\color{main} \frac{n(n+1)}{2}}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^{2} = {\color{main}\frac{n(n+1)}{2}} \cdot \frac{2n+1}{3}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} i^{3} = {\color{main}\frac{n(n+1)}{2}} \cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Riemann sums and the definite integral} \label{subsec:defint-formal}
Definition~\ref{def:riemann-sum} describes a major concept in integration called the Riemann sum.

\begin{definition} \label{def:riemann-sum}
  The \hlmain{Riemann sum} for a function \(f(x)\) defined over an interval \([a,b]\) is
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:riemann-sum} \Huge
    \sum_{{\color{main}i}=1}^{{\color{main}n}} f(x_{{\color{main}i}}^{*}) \; {\color{supp} \Delta x}.
  \end{equation}

  If \(f(x)\) is \emph{continuous} and non-negative on \([a,b]\), then \hlmain{the area under the curve \(y = f(x)\) on \([a,b]\)} is defined to be
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:area-under-the-curve}
    \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*}) \; \Delta x.
  \end{equation}

  \textbook{The definitions on page 519}

  \updatenote{The limit moved out of the Riemann sum. Was: the Riemann sum is \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*}) \Delta x\), and the area is \enquote{the value of the corresponding Riemann sum}. Why: a Riemann sum is usually the finite sum, an approximation using \(n\) rectangles. Its limit is the area, and Definition~\ref{def:definite-integral} below uses the same limit, so before this change one expression had two names.}
\end{definition}

\blanklines{10}

\(n\) is a constant inside the summation. The other symbols are explained on page~\pageref{page:riemann-notation}. \updatenote{The rest of the list that was here moved to page~\pageref{page:riemann-notation}. Why: the approximation methods use it before this definition.}

Continuity guarantees the choice of \(x_{i}^{*}\) does not matter at all. We are free to choose anything we want from \([x_{i-1}, x_{i}]\) to be our \(x_{i}^{*}\). From a practical point of view, it means that as long as we approximate with enough rectangles we will eventually get more and more accurate answers, regardless of how we choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) (could just be a random choice). \updatenote{Moved from before the five approximation methods. Why: it is about the limit as \(n \to \infty\), so it belongs after the limit is defined.}

\begin{example}
  Express the area under the curve \(y = x^{2}\) over \([0,1]\) as a limit of Riemann sums. \updatenote{Was \enquote{as a Riemann sum}. Why: the area is now the limit of Riemann sums, not a Riemann sum itself.}

  \blanklines{10}
\end{example}
\clearpage

The freedom to choose \(x_{i}^{*}\) is important for approximation. In practice, \hlmain{choosing \(x_{i}^{*}\) to be \(x_{i}\) (the right endpoints) always gives us notationally simpler expressions} for finding area under the curve using definition.

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:area-under-parabola}
  Use the definition to find the area under the curve \(y = x^{2}\) over the interval \([0,1]\).

  As a reminder, the area as defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:area-under-the-curve} is \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*}) \Delta x\).

  \blanklines{40}
\end{exercise}
\updatenote{Uncommented this paragraph and exercise, corrected \enquote{always give} to \enquote{always gives}, and changed \enquote{the Riemann sum as defined in} to \enquote{the area as defined in}. Why: the exercise answers the question that opened Subsection~\ref{subsec:approximating-area}, and the summation formulas it needs are now in the same lesson.}
\clearpage

Everything we did in approximating areas using rectangles leads to the definite integral, which is defined for all functions, not just the non-negative ones. \updatenote{Added a comma before the non-restrictive ``which'' clause.}
\begin{definition} \label{def:definite-integral}
  The \hlmain{definite integral of a function \(f(x)\) \hlsupp{from \(a\) to \(b\)}} is defined by
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral}
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*}) \Delta x.
  \end{equation}
  \hlsupp{provided} that the limit exists. If this limit exists, then we say \hlmain{\(f\) is integrable on \([a, b]\)} or is an integrable function.
\end{definition}

This limit makes the net area in Definition~\ref{def:definite-integral-net-area} precise. In particular, Exercise~\ref{ex:area-under-parabola} shows \(\int_{0}^{1} x^{2} \;dx = \underline{\hspace{1cm}}\). \updatenote{New paragraph. Why: it connects the formal definition back to the net area meaning in Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-meaning}, and closes the question that motivated approximation.}

\bigskip
Because the definite integral is defined in terms of a summation, all but one of its properties mimic those of summation properties on page~\pageref{page:summation-properties}. \updatenote{"that of" corrected to "those of" to agree with the plural "properties".} \updatenote{Moved here from the start of the properties, which are now in Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-properties}. Why: that subsection explains the properties with net area. Once summations are available, this sentence gives a second reason they hold.}
The flip property, Equation~\eqref{eq:definite-integral-flip}, \hlwarn{does not} have a counterpart in summations.
Recall summations have a split/merge property \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} + \sum_{i=m+1}^{n} a_{i}\) restricted to \(1 \le m \le n\).
The split/merge property of definite integrals, Equation~\eqref{eq:definite-integral-split}, has \hlmain{no restriction} at all.
\updatenote{These comparisons with summations moved here from the flip and split/merge properties in Subsection~\ref{subsec:defint-properties}. Why: summations had not been introduced at that point.}
\clearpage
