\begin{document} \section{Definite integrals}
Everything we did in approximating areas using rectangles leads to the definite integral which is defined for all functions, not just the non-negative ones.
\begin{definition} \label{def:definite-integral}
  The \hlmain{definite integral of a function \(f(x)\) \hlsupp{from \(a\) to \(b\)}} is defined by 
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral} 
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_{i}^{*}) \Delta x.
  \end{equation}
  \hlsupp{provided} that the limit exists. If this limit exists, then we say \hlmain{\(f\) is integrable on \([a, b]\)} or is an integrable function. 

  The definite integral \(\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx\) represents the \hlmain{net area bounded by \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = 0\)} \hlsupp{over the interval \([a,b]\)}.  Sometimes, it is also called the net signed area.
\end{definition}
\[
  \text{net area} = \bigg(\text{area below \(y = f(x)\) and above \(y = 0\)}\bigg) - \bigg(\text{area above \(y = f(x)\) and below \(y = 0\)}\bigg).
\]

We now study properties of definite integrals. Because the definite integral is defined in terms of a summation, all but one of its properties mimic that of summation properties on page~\pageref{page:summation-properties}.

\begin{thm}
  \begin{align*}
    \int_{a}^{b} (\text{constant}) f(x) \;dx &= (\text{constant}) \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx && \text{(constant multiple)}\\[1ex]
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx + \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \;dx &= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) + g(x) \;dx && \text{(sum)} \\[1ex]
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx - \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \;dx &= \int_{a}^{b} f(x) - g(x) \;dx && \text{(difference)}
  \end{align*}

  \textbook{Equations (5.13), (5.11) and (5.12) on page 538}
\end{thm}
\clearpage

Definite integrals have a ``flip'' property (non-standard terminology) which \hlwarn{does not} have a counterpart in summations. 

\begin{thm}
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral-flip}
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = - \int_{b}^{a} f(x) \;dx.
  \end{equation}

  \textbook{Equation (5.10) on page 538}
\end{thm}

\blanklines{10}

\faStar{} All remaining properties of definite integrals come from their net area interpretation.

\begin{thm}
  \begin{equation}
    \int_{a}^{a} f(x) \;dx = \underline{\hspace{1cm}}.
  \end{equation}

  \textbook{Equation (5.9) on page 537}
\end{thm}

Recall summations have a split/merge property \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{i} = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_{i} + \sum_{i=m+1}^{n} a_{i}\) restricted to \(1 \le m \le n\).
Definite integrals also have a split/merge property but with \hlmain{no restriction} at all.

\begin{thm}
  \begin{equation} \label{eq:definite-integral-split}
    \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \;dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \;dx.
  \end{equation}

  The number \(c\) can be anything, even outside the interval \([a,b]\), as long as both integrals on the right are well-defined.

  \textbook{Equation (5.14) on page 538}
\end{thm}
\blanklines{10}
\clearpage

The net area interpretation of a definite integral yields two comparison properties. For each part below, fill in the blanks with one of \(=, \le , \ge\) by interpreting definite integrals as net area.
\begin{enumerate}[wide, noitemsep]
  \item If \(f(x) \le g(x)\) over \([a,b]\), then
    \[
      \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx \underline{\hspace{2cm}} \int_{a}^{b} g(x) \;dx.
    \]

    \blanklines{8}

  \item If \(m\) and \(M\) are constants such that \(m \le f(x) \le M\) on \([a,b]\), then
    \[
      m(b-a) \underline{\hspace{2cm}} \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \underline{\hspace{2cm}} M(b-a).
    \]

    \blanklines{8}
\end{enumerate}

We wrap up the discussion of the geometric interpretation of definite integrals by defining the average value of a function. 

\blanklines{15}

The average value of a continuous \(f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) is
\[
  f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b-a} \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \;dx.
\]
