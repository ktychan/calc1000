\begin{document} \section{The concept of limits of a function}
A limit describes the pattern of a function when its \emph{independent variable} moves closer to a constant

\faLightbulb{} To \emph{estimate} \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\), we move \(x\) closer and closer to the constant \(a\) (but not touching \(a\)) from both sides and look for a pattern: Does \(f(x)\) eventually get arbitrarily closer to some constant \(L\)? 

\begin{center}
  If such an \(L\) \hlmain{exists}, then \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\); otherwise, the limit \(\lim_{x \to a}f(x)\) \hlmain{does not exist}.
\end{center}

\faComments{} Suppose we have a function \(f(x)\), and we know \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) exists. What do we know about \(f(a)\)?
\blanklines{5}

To evaluate \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\), we approach the constant \(a\) \hlinfo{from both sides}. Why not just look at it \hlsupp{one side at a time}?  Let's define \hlmain{one-sided limits}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item If we can make \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to a constant \(L\) by moving \(x\) closer to \(a\) \hlmain{from the left}, then we say \(\lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = L\); otherwise, \(\lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x)\) does not exist.

  \item If we can make \(f(x)\) arbitrarily close to a constant \(L\) by moving \(x\) closer to \(a\) \hlmain{from the right}, then we say \(\lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = L\); otherwise, \(\lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x)\) does not exist.
\end{itemize}

\blanklines{2}

\begin{example}
  Let's look at \(g_{1}(x)\) and \(g_{2}(x)\) again.
  Estimate the following one-sided limits.

  \begin{align*}
    \lim_{x \to 1^{-}} g_{1}(x)  \;
      & \underline{\hspace{2cm}}
      & \lim_{x \to 1^{-}} g_{2}(x)  \;
      & \underline{\hspace{2cm}} \\[2ex]
      \lim_{x \to 1^{+}} g_{1}(x)  \;
      & \underline{\hspace{2cm}}
      & \lim_{x \to 1^{+}} g_{2}(x)  \;
      & \underline{\hspace{2cm}}
  \end{align*}
\end{example}

\clearpage

\begin{thm} \label{thm:limit-two-one-sides}
  Suppose \(f(x)\) is defined at all values in an open interval containing some constant \(a\) with the possible exception of \(a\) itself, and let \(L\) be a real number. Then
  \[
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = L \text{ and } \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = L.
  \]

  \textbook{Theorem 2.2 on page 145}
\end{thm}

The third bullet point following Theorem~\ref{thm:limit-two-one-sides} says that a cause for which a limit fails to exist is when two one-sided limits do not equal each other. On this page, we introduce two more causes for which a limit does not exist.

\bigskip

The second cause is oscillation as demonstrated in Example~\ref{eg:limit-oscillation}.  Examples~\ref{eg:limit-oscillation} and \ref{eg:limit-oscillation-nonexample} can be hard to come up with without a lot of trial and error. You are recommended to memorize them.

\begin{example} \label{eg:limit-oscillation}
  The function \(\color{supp} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\) oscillates near \(0\). Therefore, 
  \[
    \lim_{x \to 0} {\color{supp} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)} \text{ does not exist.}
  \]

  Graph: \url{https://www.desmos.com/calculator/cbosu9tn22}
\end{example}

\begin{example} \label{eg:limit-oscillation-nonexample}
  Compare Example~\ref{eg:limit-oscillation} to \(\lim_{x \to 0} \color{main} x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)\). The two limits look very similar. However, we can estimate (see Exercise~\ref{ex:limit-law-x-sin(x)} for a relevant algebraic technique) that 
  \[
    \lim_{x \to 0} {\color{main} x \sin \left( \frac{1}{x} \right)} = 0.
  \]

  Graph: \url{https://www.desmos.com/calculator/c9oi2qk5kw}
\end{example}

\begin{definition}
  A vertical line \(x = a\) is called a \hlmain{vertical asymptote} of \(f(x)\) if
  \[
    \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = \pm \infty \qquad\text{ or }\qquad \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = \pm \infty.
  \]

  \textbook{page 149}
\end{definition}

\faExclamationTriangle{} Vertical asymptotes have a subtle trap: Roots of the denominator are \hlwarn{not always} vertical asymptotes.  To avoid making subtle mistakes, always use the definition above to identify vertical asymptotes.

\end{exercise}
