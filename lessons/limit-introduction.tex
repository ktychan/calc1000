\begin{document} \section{The concept of limit at a number}
The concept of limit describes the pattern of a function when its \emph{independent variable} moves closer closer to a constant \(a\) but never touching \(a\). In this lesson, we study three related concepts.
\[
  \underbrace{\lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x)}_{\text{left limit}}, 
  \quad 
  \underbrace{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)}_{\text{two-sided limit}},
  \quad 
  \underbrace{\lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x)}_{\text{right limit}}.
\]

To \emph{estimate} (or visually evaluate) one of the three above limits, move \(x\) towards \(a\) (depending on the direction, but never actually touching \(a\)) and answer the question:
\begin{center}
  \faStar{} \hlattn{Does \(f(x)\) eventually get arbitrarily closer to some constant \(L\)?}
\end{center}
If it does, we say \hlmain{\(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) exists and is equal to \(L\)}; otherwise, we say \hlsupp{\(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) does not exist}.

In high school, we look for vertical asymptotes by looking for roots of the denominator. This concept \hlattn{expands} now that we understand limits.
\begin{definition} \label{def:vertical-asymptote}
  A vertical line \(x = a\) is called a \hlmain{vertical asymptote} of \(f(x)\) if
  \[
    \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = \pm \infty \qquad\text{ or }\qquad \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = \pm \infty.
  \]

  \textbook{page 149}
\end{definition}
\clearpage

The relation between one-sided limits and two-sided limit we observed in Example~\ref{eg:two-sided-limits} is always true. We summarize this relation in Theorem~\ref{thm:limit-two-one-sides}.

\begin{thm} \label{thm:limit-two-one-sides}
  Suppose \(f(x)\) is defined at all values in an open interval containing some constant \(a\) with the possible exception of \(a\) itself, and let \(L\) be a real number. Then
  \[
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = L \text{ and } \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = L.
  \]

  \textbook{Theorem 2.2 on page 145}
\end{thm}

\textbf{Summary of the fundamentals of limits}.
We learned three fundamental concepts of limits at a constant \(a\) and their relationships.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item To \hlmain{evaluate} a \emph{left} or \emph{right limit} as \(x\) approaches \(a\) is to determine which of the three cases below applies to the function \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\) from the left or right. 

    \begin{center}
      \includestandalone[page=8]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
      \qquad
      \includestandalone[page=10]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
      \qquad
      \includestandalone[page=12]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
    \end{center}

  \item Remember Theorem~\ref{thm:limit-two-one-sides} and that vertical asymptotes are \emph{defined} in terms of one-sided limits.

  \item To \hlmain{evaluate} a \emph{two-sided limit} as \(x\) approaches \(a\) is to determine whether its two one-sided limits are equal.  This is supported by the Theorem~\ref{thm:limit-two-one-sides} on page~\pageref{thm:limit-two-one-sides}.  Also remember that the function value \(f(a)\) has nothing to do with \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\). 
    \begin{itemize}
      \item If the two one-sided limits are equal, then the two-sided limit equals that common value (which can be a finite number or \(\pm \infty\)).
        \begin{center}
          \includestandalone[page=33]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
          \qquad
          \includestandalone[page=35]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
        \end{center}
      \item If the two one-sided limits are different, then the two-sided limit does not exist.
        \begin{center}
          \includestandalone[page=36]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
          \quad
          \includestandalone[page=37]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
          \quad
          \includestandalone[page=38]{../standalones/plot-limit-intro}
        \end{center}
    \end{itemize}
  \item When \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty\), the \enquote{\(=\infty\)} is only a shorthand for \enquote{limit approaches infinity}, and the limit does not exist.  The same is true for \enquote{\(=-\infty\)}.
\end{enumerate}
