\begin{document} \section{Reading week}
The reading week offers some time for reflection.  Here is some food for thought.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{How often you practise a technique may affect how well you can recall it in an exam.} Suppose you work on 10 practice problems on a single topic, say limits. You notice that every pair of them shares a shortcut. You end up practising each shortcut twice. Alternatively, you apply the same systematic (but longer) approach to every problem, so you practise that one approach 10 times.

    \begin{center}
      \includestandalone[page=1]{../standalones/study-skills}
    \end{center}
    Quiz questions are grouped so that, hopefully, you see the benefit of a systematic approach. \hlattn{You are more likely to remember the technique you practised 10 times than the shortcuts you practised only twice.}

  \item \textbf{The order you practise in matters too.} As you move closer to exam time, you need to practise \hlmain{multiple techniques} in short succession to practise \hlattn{how to choose an effective technique} for a problem.

    \begin{center}
      \includestandalone[page=2]{../standalones/study-skills}
    \end{center}

    \hlattn{Map out the techniques and when to use them. List all
      examples, exercises and quiz problems that use each technique.} Refer back to your map often to develop proficiency and consistency. Ultimately, the goal is to recall and use your map during an exam.

    You may use the index-card method. Whenever you finish a practice problem, write down the problem on one side of an index card and the relevant techniques on the other side. During an exam prep session, shuffle the cards and practise identifying the relevant techniques for each problem. Alternatively, give an AI the entire set of lecture notes (with or without your own notes) and ask it to pick out a random set of problems.

  \item \textbf{Priming} uses \hlmain{active recall} to pull in relevant facts for \hlattn{problems of the same type} before attempting them, so that your working (short term) memory is filled with relevant facts. Its effectiveness depends on the completeness of your map of knowledge.

  \item \textbf{Ease your mental load during an exam.} Exam questions are not listed in any particular order. Read through the exam and group questions by topic. For example, do all the limit questions first, then all the continuity questions, and so on. If you work from the first question to the last, you may have to tackle concepts in a random order.

    Use \hlattn{priming} to prepare yourself before attempting each type of problem. This way, you can focus on one topic at a time and ease your mental load. Use \href{https://westernu.brightspace.com/d2l/le/lessons/201580/units/3922747}{\color{link} past midterms \footnotesize\faExternalLink*} to experiment and find what works well. For example, you may prefer priming before looking for all the limit questions. Alternatively, maybe you prefer finding all the limit questions, priming, then attempting them. Lastly, there is a time cost to priming. Maybe you prefer to prime just once, right before the test.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-exercise-4}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2022)
  Suppose \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x - 1}\). How many discontinuities does the function \(f(f(x))\) have?
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(f(x)\) is continuous everywhere.
    \item Exactly one.
    \item Exactly two.
    \item Exactly three.
    \item More than three.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    Simplify a complex and unfamiliar expression. Notice that
    \[
      f(f(x)) = \frac{1}{\frac{1}{x-1} - 1}.
    \]

    The systematic approach to finding discontinuities is to start with the three tasks in Definition~\ref{def:continuity} on page~\pageref{def:continuity}. For what values of \(a\) is \(f(a)\) not defined? \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) does not exist? \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) \ne f(a)\)?  The number of discontinuities is the number of \(a\) for which at least one of the three tasks fails.
  \end{hint}
  \begin{solution}
    This exercise involves a mix of domain, function composition, finding discontinuities (a subtask of classifying \emph{dis}continuities discussed in class on page~\pageref{technique:classify-discontinuities}).

    Carry out the composition to get a concrete expression \(\frac{1}{\frac{1}{x-1} - 1}\). We can see a discontinuity \(x = 1\) because the inner quotient cannot be \(0\) in the denominator.  The zeros of the denominator of the whole quotient give us the remaining discontinuities.  We solve for \(x\) in \(\frac{1}{x - 1} - 1 = 0\). Therefore, \(x = 1\) and \(x = 2\) are the only discontinuities.

    We would have missed the discontinuity at \(x = 1\) had we immediately manipulated \(f(f(x))\) to \(\frac{x-1}{2-x}\).  Remember, some algebraic operations change the domain of the function.
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-exercise-7}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2023) 
  Find all values of \(c\) for which the function
  \[
    f(x) =
    \begin{cases}
      \frac{|x - 3|}{x - c}, &\text{if } x \ne c \\
      1, &\text{if } x = c
    \end{cases}
  \]
  is continuous on \((-\infty, \infty)\).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item There are no such \(c\).
    \item \(c = 3\).
    \item All real numbers except \(3\).
    \item \(c = 0\).
    \item All real numbers except \(0\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    This is a very complex problem. The mind map on page~\pageref{page:mindmap-limits-full} essentially guides us through it.

    \begin{itemize}
      \item Start at the \enquote{continuity at \(a\)} node.

        Systematically, go down the list of three tasks in the definition of continuity on page~\pageref{def:continuity}.  The first two tasks are easily satisfied.  The third task requires the following equation
        \[
          \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)
        \]
        to be true for the unknown \(c\).

      \item Write down the concrete expressions for both sides to see what we are dealing with.
        \[
          \lim_{x \to c} \frac{|x-3|}{x-c} = 1.
        \]

      \item In every example of such a problem (see Example~\ref{eg:continuity-solve-simple} on page~\pageref{eg:continuity-solve-simple} and the rest of Section~\ref{sec:solve-for-unknowns}), we attempted to evaluate the limit. We do the same here starting at the \enquote{try direct substitution} node in the mind map.

        There are two possibilities.
        \begin{itemize}
          \item If \(c \ne 3\), then we have \(\frac{\text{nonzero}}{0}\) which does not exist and \(f(x)\) is not continuous at \(c\). So \(c\) must be \(3\).
          \item If \(c = 3\), then we have a concrete equation
            \[
              \lim_{x \to 3} \frac{|x-3|}{x-3} = 1.
            \]
            We can directly evaluate the limit on the left-hand side using one-sided limits. The slightly faster way to do so is to recognize that the limit on the left-hand side is similar to
            \begin{itemize}
              \item Part (b) of Problem 4 in Quiz 2,
              \item Part (d) of Problem 4 in Quiz 2, and
              \item Example~\ref{eg:derivative-absolute-value} on page~\pageref{eg:derivative-absolute-value}, where the derivative of \(|x-1|\) at \(1\) is the limit \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|h|}{h}\).
            \end{itemize}

            Being able to recall these results helps you to quickly conclude that the limit on the left-hand side does not exist.  So \(c\) cannot be \(3\) either.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

    Finally, we conclude that there is no choice of \(c\) for which the function \(f(x)\) is continuous at \(c\).
  \end{hint}
  \begin{solution}
    The function is continuous everywhere except possibly at \(c\). The \hlwarn{definition} of continuity at \(c\) gives us an equation \(\lim_{x \to c} \frac{|x - 3|}{x - c} = f(c)\) involving the unknown \(c\). Starting from this equation, we are going to draw a conclusion about the continuity at \(c\).  Recall the three tasks discussed immediately below the definition of continuity on page~\pageref{def:continuity}.

    The right-hand side is \(1\).  The left-hand side requires two separate analyses.
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Case \(c \ne 3\).  Then \(\lim_{x \to c} \frac{|x - 3|}{x - c}\) is of type \(\frac{\text{non-zero constant}}{0}\) which does not exist.  See Example~\ref{eg:limit-constant-over-0} for a reference.

      \item Case \(c = 3\). Notice \(\lim_{x \to c} \frac{|x - 3|}{x - c}\) is of type \(\frac{0}{0}\). We learned on page~\pageref{eg:limit-cancellation} that limits of this type require more work. The piecewise nature of the function suggests that Equation~\eqref{eq:abs} on page~\pageref{eq:abs} and Example~\ref{eg:piecewise-abs-of-function} on page~\pageref{eg:piecewise-abs-of-function} are relevant. We perform the following sequence of calculations.  Start by applying the techniques discussed on page~\pageref{eq:abs} to write \(|x - 3|\) as a piecewise function.
        \[
          |x - 3| =
          \begin{cases}
            x - 3 & \text{if } x \ge 3 \\
            -(x - 3) & \text{if } x < 3.
          \end{cases}
        \]
        Divide both branches by \(x - 3\) to get a concrete expression
        \[
          \frac{|x - 3|}{{\color{main}x-3}}
          =
          \begin{cases}
            \frac{x-3}{{\color{main}x-3}} &\text{if } x > 3 \\
            \frac{-(x-3)}{{\color{main}x-3}} &\text{if } x < 3
          \end{cases}
          =
          \begin{cases}
            1 &\text{if } x > 3 \\
            -1 &\text{if } x < 3
          \end{cases}
          .
        \]

        The limit approaches the boundary of the two branches. The two one-sided limits are quite obviously not equal. Therefore, the two-sided limit does not exist.
    \end{enumerate}

    The two-sided limit at \(c\) does not exist in either case.  There is no choice of \(c\) for which the equation \(\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)\) is true. Therefore, the function cannot be continuous at \(c\) regardless the choice of \(c\).
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-practice-1}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2019)
  The logarithmic function \(f(x) = \log_{a}(x) + b\) whose graph passes through points \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) is
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(\ln(e^{2}x)\)
    \item \(\log_{1/2}\left(\frac{x}{4}\right) + 1\)
    \item \(\log_{1/2}(x) + 2\)
    \item \(\log_{2}(x) - 1\)
    \item \(\log_{2}(x) + 2\)
  \end{enumerate}
  \begin{hint}
    This is similar to Problem 11, Quiz 1 on WebWork.
  \end{hint}

  \begin{solution}
    We need equations involving the unknowns \(a\) and \(b\). The points \(f(x)\) passes through give us two equations.
    \[
      \left\{
      \begin{array}{rcl}
        f(1) &=& 2\\
        f(2) &=& 1
      \end{array}
      \right.
      \longrightarrow
      \left\{
      \begin{array}{rcl}
        \log_{a}(1) + b &=& 2\\
        \log_{a}(2) + b &=& 1
      \end{array}
      \right.
      \overset{\text{\tiny \(\log_{a}(1) = 0\)}}{\longrightarrow}
      \left\{
      \begin{array}{rcl}
        b &=& 2\\
        \log_{a}(2) + 2 &=& 1
      \end{array}
      \right.
      \overset{\text{\tiny see below}}{\longrightarrow}
      \left\{
      \begin{array}{rcl}
        b &=& 2\\
        a &=& 1/2
      \end{array}
      \right.
    \]
    In the last step, we solve for \(a\) in \(\log_{a}(2) = -1\). The fast method is to raise both sides to base \(a\) to get \(2 = a^{-1}\) and then \(a = 1/2\).  The systematic method (adds just one step) is to change base to \(e\) to get \(\frac{\ln(2)}{\ln(a)} = -1\) which turns into \(-\ln(2) = \ln(a)\). Raise both sides to \(e\) to get \(a = 2^{-1}\).

    Alternatively, if you prefer exponentials over logarithms, start by converting \(f(x) = \log_{a}(x) + b\) into exponentials by raising both sides to \(a\) to get \(a^{f(x)} = x a^{b}\). Notice it is quite tempting to make an algebra mistake here. Plug in the point \((1,2)\) to get \(a^{2} = 1 a^{b}\) which means \(b = 2\). Plug in the point \((2,1)\) and \(b = 2\) to get \(a^{1} = 2 a^{2}\). Manipulate the exponents to get \(a = 2^{-1}\).
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-exercise-8}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2023)
  If \(\lim_{x \to 8^{-}} f(x) = 1\), \(\lim_{x \to 8^{+}} f(x) = 1\), and \(f(8) = -1\), then which of the following statements is true?
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(f\) is continuous at \(8\).
    \item \(\lim_{x \to 8} f(x)\) exists and \(f\) is discontinuous at \(8\).
    \item \(\lim_{x \to 8} f(x)\) does not exist and \(f\) is discontinuous at \(8\).
    \item \(f\) is differentiable at \(8\).
    \item \(f'(8) = 0\).
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    Sketch the given information as we did in Example~\ref{eg:discontinuity-classify-abstract} on page~\pageref{eg:discontinuity-classify-abstract}.
  \end{hint}

  \begin{solution}
    This is about Theorem~\ref{thm:limit-two-one-sides} on page~\pageref{thm:limit-two-one-sides} and the \hlwarn{definition} of continuity on page~\pageref{def:continuity}. The given left and right limits at \(8\) are equal. Therefore, \(\lim_{x \to 8} f(x)\) does exist. The \hlwarn{definition} for continuity at \(8\) requires \(\lim_{x \to 8} f(x) = f(8)\) to be true.  Therefore, the limit does exist but \(f\) is discontinuous at \(8\).
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

  % (Midterm, Fall 2024)
  Evaluate \(\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)\) if \(0 \le f(x) \le |x \sin(1/x)|\) for all \(x\).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]

    The only information available for \(f(x)\) is an inequality.  That's a sign for the squeeze theorem as demonstrated in Exercise~\ref{ex:limit-squeeze-easy}. The bounds are \(0 \le | x \sin(1/x)| \le |x|\). The limit of the upper bound function \(|x|\) as \(x \to 0\) is \(0\). Voil\`a! The squeeze theorem says \(\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 0\).
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-practice-13}
  Evaluate \(\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt[3]{x} - 1}{\sqrt{x} - 1}\).
  Write your solution in the box provided below.

  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \draw (0,0) rectangle (6,2);
    \node[right] at (0.5, 0.5) {The limit is \underline{\hspace{2cm}}};
  \end{tikzpicture}

  \begin{hint}
    Attempt a direct substitution to get \(0/0\) and use a change of variable \(u = x^{1/6}\) to turn the unfamiliar problem into a familiar one.
  \end{hint}

  \begin{solution}
    This is about using a change of variable to turn an unfamiliar problem into a familiar one.
    Substitute \(u = x^{1/6}\). As \(x \to 1\), \(u\) approaches the ``happy face'' \(\lim_{x \to 1} x^{1/6} = 1\). Moreover \(x^{1/3} = u^{2}\) and \(x^{1/2} = u^{3}\). After all substitutions, we arrive at
    \[
      \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{\sqrt[3]{x}-1}{\sqrt{x}-1} \overset{\text{sub \(u = x^{1/6}\)}}{=} \lim_{u \to 1} \frac{u^{2} - 1}{u^{3} - 1} = \lim_{u \to 1} \frac{\cancel{(u-1)}(u+1)}{\cancel{(u-1)}(u^{2}+u+1)} = \frac{2}{3}.
    \]
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-practice-2}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2019) 
  If \(f(x) = 2(\ln(x))^{3}\), then the inverse function \(f^{-1}(x)\) is
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(e^{\sqrt[3]{x/2}}\)
    \item \(\sqrt[3]{e^{x/2}}\)
    \item \(2(e^{x})^{3}\)
    \item \(2e^{x/2}\)
    \item The inverse function does not exist.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    Find the inverse function by isolating for \(y\) in the equation \(x = f(y)\) which comes from applying \(f(x)\) to both sides of \(y = f^{-1}(x)\).
  \end{hint}
  \begin{solution}
    This is a mix of the fundamentals of inverse functions, the equivalent equation in particular, and the inverse pair \(e^{x}\) and \(\ln(x)\).  Start by writing down the equivalent equation for \(y = f^{-1}(x)\), as discussed in Example~\ref{eg:find-inverse}, which is \(x = 2(\ln(y))^{3}\). Isolate for \(y\) as follows:
    \[
      \frac{x}{2} = \left( \ln(y) \right)^{3} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \sqrt[3]{x/2} = \ln(y) \quad\longrightarrow\quad e^{\sqrt[3]{x/2}} = y.
    \]
    It is quite easy to make the following mistake: \(\Big(\ln(x)\Big)^{3} = 3\ln(x)\). The correct logarithm law is \(\ln(x^{a}) = a\ln(x)\). Brackets matter!
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}
\nopagebreak\vfill

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-practice-11}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2023)
  Let \(f(x) = \begin{cases} x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right) &\text{if } x < 0 \\ 2x &\text{if } x \ge 0\end{cases}\), then
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(f\) is not defined at \(0\).
    \item \(f\) is defined at \(0\) but has no limit there.
    \item \(f\) is differentiable at \(0\) but not continuous there.
    \item \(f\) is differentiable at \(0\)
    \item \(f\) is continuous at \(0\) but not differentiable there.
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    The systematic approach is to work your way up from the most fundamental concept starting at the domain. This is simply five problems squished into a single multiple-choice problem.
  \end{hint}
  \begin{solution}
    Work your way down the list.
    \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
      \item The function is defined at \(0\) in the second branch.
      \item The left limit is \(\lim_{x \to 0^{-}} x \sin(x^{-2}) = 0\) by the squeeze theorem because \(-x \ge x \sin(x^{-2}) \ge x\).  The right limit is \(0\). The two-sided limit at \(0\) exists.
    \end{enumerate}


    The next three options are all about the derivative at \(0\). It cannot be C because continuity is a critical ingredient of differentiation as discussed on page~\pageref{thm:differentiability-implies-continuity}.  It comes down to either D or E.

    The number \(0\) sits at the boundary of the two branches. Therefore, we are forced to work with the \hlwarn{definition} of the derivative at \(0\) as two one-sided limits.
    \[
      \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \frac{h\sin(1/h^{2})}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^{-}} \sin(1/h^{2}) \overset{\text{sub } u = h^{2}}{=} \lim_{u \to 0^{+}} \sin(1/u).
    \]
    The sine term cycles between \(-1\) and \(1\) ever faster as \(u \to 0^{+}\), so it settles on no value. Therefore, the left limit does not exist.

    No need to go any further for this particular exercise. The derivative at \(0\) does not exist by the \hlwarn{definition} of the derivative at \(0\).
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise} \label{ex:midterm-exercise-5}
  % (Midterm, Fall 2022)
  Solve for \(x\) in the equation \(\ln(x) + \ln(x - 1) = 0\).
  \begin{enumerate}[label=\Alph*.]
    \item \(\frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}\)
    \item \(\frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}, \frac{1 - \sqrt{5}}{2}\)
    \item \(\frac{-1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}\)
    \item \(\frac{-1 - \sqrt{5}}{2}\)
    \item \(\frac{5}{2}\)
  \end{enumerate}

  \begin{hint}
    Apply logarithm rules to simplify the problem.
  \end{hint}

  \begin{solution}
    This exercise is about properties (rules) of exponential functions and logarithms.  It is entirely an exercise in remembering the rules correctly and remembering that \(e^{x}\) and \(\ln(x)\) are inverses of each other. They ``cancel out.'' See the top of page~\pageref{eq:log-formulas} for a refresher on the logarithm rules.

    Use the logarithm rule \(\ln(ab) = \ln(a) + \ln(b)\). \[\ln(x) + \ln(x - 1) = 0 \quad\longrightarrow\quad \ln(x(x-1)) = 0 \quad\longrightarrow\quad x^{2} - x = 1.\] In the last step, we raise both sides to base \(e\).  Finish the calculation using the quadratic formula or completing the square. Pick only the positive one because \(\ln(\cdots)\) does not accept negative numbers as inputs.
  \end{solution}
\end{exercise}
